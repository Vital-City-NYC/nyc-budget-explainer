How "Who pays for New York City" was built

The sources, the methods and every judgment call behind the $100 revenue explainer, plus the four rounds of checking it has been through. Updated October 22, 2026.

What the page shows

The page turns each year's city revenue into 100 squares, one per $1 of every $100, for fiscal 2000 through 2027. It answers three questions: where the money comes from, who is billed for each tax and how much of it the mayor can change without Albany.

  • The money. General Fund revenue only, the city's main operating account. Fiscal 2000 to 2025 is actual revenue from the Comptroller's annual comprehensive financial reports. Fiscal 2026 and 2027 are budget plans from the Office of Management and Budget: 2026 as modified in June 2026, 2027 as adopted.
  • Fiscal 2027 total: $126.2 billion, which equals budgeted spending because the budget must balance. The Comptroller and news reports cite $125.8 billion, which nets out a $400 million adjustment for prior-year bills.
  • Eight buckets: property tax, personal income tax, sales tax, business taxes, other taxes, state aid, federal aid and fees, fines and other.
  • Who pays, not who bears. The page shows who is billed. It does not estimate who finally carries a tax through rents, prices, wages or investment returns.
  • Rounding. Squares are allocated by largest remainder so they always add to 100, and the whole-dollar figures on the page are those square counts; hovering a square gives the share to one decimal. A source can show one square more or fewer than plain rounding would give.

Limits

The page describes billing and legal authority in one year's budget; it does not measure fairness, burden or capacity.

  • No incidence. Landlords may pass property tax to tenants, and businesses may pass taxes to customers or workers. The city's own Advisory Commission on Property Tax Reform found the evidence on renters inconclusive, so the page does not guess.
  • Mismatched years. The revenue is fiscal 2000 to 2027; the payer tables are fiscal 2026, tax year 2023 and tax year 2022. Each table says which.
  • General Fund only. Capital spending, off-budget authorities and the transit authority's own taxes are outside the squares.
  • Two property tax totals. The budget office prints $37.19 billion for fiscal 2027; the Comptroller's reproduction of the same plan shows $37.30 billion because it adds the state's school tax relief reimbursement. The page uses the budget office's figure.
  • Residents only. The income tax table covers 3.9 million resident returns. Commuters do not pay city income tax.
  • "Utilities" is Class 3 only. Utility companies' land and buildings are Class 4 and so sit in the commercial row.
  • Kept off the page as unverified: the Citizens Budget Commission's effective tax rates by property type (its site blocked automated reading), and the state enabling law behind the hotel tax rate.

Open questions

  • One city-controlled number? A line-by-line sort of the mixed groups (October 19, not on the page) supports a single figure: about $35 of every $100 in fiscal 2027 comes from taxes and charges whose rates City Hall and the City Council set, or about $33 leaving out water and sewer charges, which only repay the cost of the water system. The weakest calls in that sort total under 30 cents per $100.
  • Which law extended the city income tax rates. The page says the current rates run through tax year 2029. The state's consolidated Tax Law, as revised June 12, 2026, says so. But the extender bill introduced at the city's request (S10609) shows only a Senate vote, so the chapter that made the change has not been identified.
  • Refresh when the fiscal 2027 property tax report comes out. The class table is fiscal 2026. The Comptroller's fiscal 2026 report, due about October 31, 2026, will let fiscal 2026 move from a budget plan to actual revenue.
