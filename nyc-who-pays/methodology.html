How the budget explainer was built

The sources, the methods and the judgment calls behind all four parts: who pays, where it goes, how spending has changed since 2000 and what the city builds. Updated November 19, 2026.

Each part has its own sources, judgment calls and limits, below. All four use the city's own books: the Comptroller's annual comprehensive financial reports for actual results and the Office of Management and Budget's adopted budget for fiscal 2026 and 2027. The project's research notes record the page, table or cell and a verbatim quotation for every figure.

Part one: Who pays

Open part one

What the page shows

The page turns each year's city revenue into 100 squares, one per $1 of every $100, for fiscal 2000 through 2027. It answers three questions: where the money comes from, who is billed for each tax and how much of it the city can change without Albany.

Presenting the budget as $100 follows the Independent Budget Office, whose NYC's Budget in $100 shows the fiscal 2027 adopted budget's spending that way. This project applies the device to revenue as well as spending and to actual results back to fiscal 2000, with its own groupings.

  • The money. General Fund revenue only, the city's main operating account. Fiscal 2000 to 2025 is actual revenue from the Comptroller's annual comprehensive financial reports. Fiscal 2026 and 2027 are budget plans from the Office of Management and Budget: 2026 as modified in June 2026, 2027 as adopted.
  • Fiscal 2027 total: $126.2 billion, which equals budgeted spending because the budget must balance. The Comptroller and news reports cite $125.8 billion, which nets out a $400 million adjustment for prior-year bills.
  • Eight buckets: property tax, personal income tax, sales tax, business taxes, other taxes, state aid, federal aid and fees, fines and other.
  • Who pays, not who bears. The page shows who is billed. It does not estimate who finally carries a tax through rents, prices, wages or investment returns.
  • Rounding. Squares are allocated by largest remainder so they always add to 100, and the whole-dollar figures on the page are those square counts; hovering a square gives the share to one decimal. A source can show one square more or fewer than plain rounding would give.

Judgment calls

The two biggest calls were to follow each source's own groupings rather than restate them, and to show who is billed rather than model who finally pays. The rest are below, roughly in the order a reader meets them.

Limits

The page describes billing and legal authority in one year's budget; it does not measure fairness, burden or capacity.

  • No incidence. Landlords may pass property tax to tenants, and businesses may pass taxes to customers or workers. The city's own Advisory Commission on Property Tax Reform found the evidence on renters inconclusive, so the page does not guess.
  • Different measures. The squares are revenue the city recorded; the splits are tax billed or owed. There are no splits for fiscal 2027 property tax, fiscal 2000 to 2006 or 2025 to 2027 income tax, or fiscal 2024 to 2027 business taxes, because the reports are not out. Income figures for 2014 and earlier come from samples of returns, which the Independent Budget Office calls estimates, and business figures before and after tax year 2015 cover different corporate taxes.
  • General Fund only. Capital spending, off-budget authorities and the transit authority's own taxes are outside the squares.
  • Two property tax totals. The budget office prints $37.19 billion for fiscal 2027; the Comptroller's reproduction of the same plan shows $37.30 billion because it adds the state's school tax relief reimbursement. The page uses the budget office's figure.
  • Residents only. The income tax table covers 3.9 million resident returns. Commuters do not pay city income tax.
  • “Utilities” is Class 3 only. Utility companies' land and buildings are Class 4 and so sit in the commercial row.
  • Kept off the page as unverified: the Citizens Budget Commission's effective tax rates by property type (its site blocked automated reading), and the state enabling law behind the hotel tax rate.

Limits

  • General Fund only. NYC Health + Hospitals and the New York City Housing Authority keep their own books, and the Metropolitan Transportation Authority is a state authority. The city money each receives is in the squares; the rest of their spending is not. The Water Board, which bills for water and sewer service and pays the city for running the system, is also outside the squares.
  • Budget years start small. Fiscal 2026 and 2027 will grow as grants are awarded and costs come in.
  • No causes. The share of students with disabilities, class sizes and teacher counts bear on why New York spends more per pupil than the national average; the page does not divide that gap into causes.

Limits

  • Population estimates move. Estimates for years between censuses are revised often, by up to a few percent, so per-resident figures for any one year carry that uncertainty.
  • One price index for everything. Consumer prices do not track what the city pays for its workers, supplies or buildings.

Limits

  • A national price index. The index measures state and local government construction across the country; New York City's own costs may have risen faster or slower.
  • Borrowing and spending do not match year by year. Bonds pay for work over several years.
  • Through fiscal 2025. Capital spending for fiscal 2026 and 2027 is not on the page.

Review

Every figure in the four parts was traced to a source document and then checked independently against it. Before every update the project runs a script that checks the source fingerprints, that every split adds to its parent and that the line-by-line control sort adds to part one's totals. The build scripts for parts two to four check their own totals, and the per-pupil figures are rebuilt from the source files by a script that checks the wording quotes its results.

Drafts of the explainer were shared with budget experts, and the text and charts were refined in response to their comments.

Open questions

  • One city-controlled number? A line-by-line sort of the mixed groups, not shown on the page, supports a single figure: about $35 of every $100 in fiscal 2027 comes from taxes and charges whose rates the mayor and the City Council set, or about $33 leaving out water and sewer charges, which only repay the cost of the water system. The weakest calls in that sort total under 30 cents per $100.
  • Which law extended the city income tax rates. The page says the current rates run through tax year 2029. The state's consolidated Tax Law, as revised June 12, 2026, says so. But the extender bill introduced at the city's request (S10609) shows only a Senate vote, so the chapter that made the change has not been identified.
  • Fiscal 2026 actual results. The Comptroller published its fiscal 2026 annual report on October 30, 2026. The explainer has not yet been updated with it: parts one to three still show fiscal 2026 as a budget plan, and part four ends at fiscal 2025.
  • Refresh when the fiscal 2027 property tax report comes out. Part one's class table is fiscal 2026.
